Plant sized from a rule of thumb is nearly always oversized. A proper cooling load calculation starts with good inputs.

## Outdoor and indoor design conditions

Select outdoor design dry-bulb and wet-bulb temperatures from recognised climatic data for the location, at a design percentile agreed with the client. Indoor conditions follow from the space use. A one-degree change in either shifts the result noticeably, so record them in the design basis.

## Envelope

- **Glazing** is usually the largest envelope load. Area, orientation, solar heat gain coefficient and shading all matter more than wall U-value in most commercial buildings.
- **Roof** gains are significant for top floors and single-storey buildings.
- **Walls** contribute through conduction with a time lag that depends on thermal mass.

## Internal gains

- **Occupancy:** number of people and activity level, giving sensible and latent heat.
- **Lighting:** installed power density, with allowance for controls where they are certain to be used.
- **Equipment:** realistic operating loads, not nameplate ratings. Diversity is important in offices and essential in kitchens and laboratories.

## Ventilation and infiltration

Outdoor air brought in for ventilation carries heat and, in humid climates, a large latent load. In many buildings it is the single biggest component of the cooling coil load. Fresh air rates should follow the applicable ventilation standard, and energy recovery should be evaluated where rates are high.

## From space load to plant size

1. Calculate each zone's peak load at its own peak hour.
2. Calculate the building block load at the hour when the sum is highest. It is lower than the sum of zone peaks because zones peak at different times.
3. Size terminal units on zone peaks and central plant on block load.
4. Apply a safety margin once, at the end, and state it.

## Why oversizing costs more than capital

Oversized chillers and air handling units spend their lives at low part-load, where efficiency and humidity control are poor. Accurate inputs are the cheapest energy efficiency measure available to a project.
